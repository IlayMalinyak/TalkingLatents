\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{The corpus is not split-aware.} $99.2\%$ of test-fold stars appear in
some training description. The contrast is symmetric across narrators so the paired
comparisons hold, but absolute rates are optimistic, and the adversarial set
(\Cref{tab:adversarial}) is the answer to the recall objection rather than a
clean test. A split-aware rebuild is the right fix and we have not done it.

\paragraph{The ground truth names one intruder when several qualify.} The
composer designates one off-axis member and leaves the others unconstrained on
that axis, so $60.5\%$ of anomalies sit in a group containing at least one other
member with the same label ($81.7\%$ on dwarf/giant). Naming any of them is a
true statement, but only one is scored correct. Re-scoring both narrators to credit
any qualifying member raises both and leaves the head's lead intact
($+0.361 \to +0.318$), so this does not drive the result --- but every absolute
rate in this paper is a lower bound for that reason. The same construction makes
the ``no anomaly'' groups unreliable: $71.4\%$ of them contain a member that
crosses a threshold on some axis the composer did not check, which is why groups
with no designated odd member are excluded from every number we report.

\paragraph{One seed per narrator.} Every cell was trained once. Differences of a few
points between individual cells --- particularly in \Cref{tab:basemodel} --- are
within the noise we cannot measure. The claims rest on the pattern across cells
and on effects large enough that a seed is not a plausible explanation.

\paragraph{The stellar gain is not yet attributed to the head.} The stellar
head narrator differs from the narrator without the head in its training (LoRA
instead of full fine-tuning) as well as in the token. Separating the two requires
a stellar narrator trained with LoRA and no head token, about $2{,}000$ GPU-hours
for $20$ epochs and about $5{,}000$ to match the head narrator's $50$; it has not
been run. On galaxies, where we ran this control, the training change alone
accounts for the whole gain (\Cref{tab:2x2}), so the same could be true, at least
in part, for stars. The same control would show whether the head narrator's
stability across group sizes (\Cref{fig:groupsize}) comes from the head or from
the training.

\paragraph{We can show the token is used, not how.} The ablations establish that
the token's contents are read (\Cref{tab:ablate}), but not the mechanism. Two
things remain unexplained. When the head's own top pick is wrong, the narrator
does not preferentially follow it, so it is not simply reading the head's answer
off the token. And most of the token's channels are unstable under
re-sampling --- eight of eleven have a run-to-run spread comparable to their
spread across objects --- so the information the narrator uses is probably
carried by the few channels that are stable, not by the token as a whole. We
report the effect; we do not claim to have explained it.

\paragraph{The judge is an LLM, not a human.} Population records are scored by the
judge, a $27$B model. It agrees closely with the independent rule-based verifier, which
is the evidence we have that it is not inventing its own standard, but there is
no human-labelled subset. A few hundred hand-scored groups would settle it.

\paragraph{The two tracks do not carry the same evidence.} The catalog-text
control and the adversarial set exist only for stars; galaxies have no
set-level task at all, and their relational question is cross-modal agreement
within one object rather than comparison across a group. Where a claim rests on
one track we have said so.

\paragraph{No claim about scale.} A $70$B narrator was trained on galaxies and
is excluded here: it was not a controlled comparison against the $8$B narrators
(batch $16$ vs $128$ at the same unscaled learning rate), and it degenerates
into repetition on long outputs. We therefore say nothing about how these
results move with model size.
