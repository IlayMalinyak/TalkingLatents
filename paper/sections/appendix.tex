\section{Appendix}\label{sec:appendix}

\subsection{Is the signal in the features? The ranking probe}\label{app:probe}
The group task asks which member does not belong. We test whether that is
answerable from the aligned features alone, without a language model in the
loop. For each group we rank its members by one quantity and ask where a
qualifying star lands. Two readouts are compared on exactly the same groups:
the true catalog value, and a cross-fit ridge regression that predicts the same
quantity from the aligned feature (5-fold, so no star is scored by a model that
saw it). If the probe tracks the catalog, the signal survives the alignment.

Two details matter. First, only the three continuous axes can be ranked by a
scalar; binarity and cluster membership are categorical and are excluded
($103$ of $259$ anomalies). Second, the composer designates one intruder but
leaves the other members unconstrained on that axis, so a group often contains
several stars carrying the same label --- $60.5\%$ of anomalies on the full
fold, and $81.7\%$ on dwarf/giant. Naming any of them is a true statement about
the group, so a readout is credited for the best-placed qualifying star rather
than only the designated one. ``Extreme'' is distance from the group median in
units of its median absolute deviation, which does not reveal which direction
the answer lies in.

The probe matches the catalog at rank-1 and keeps $89$--$95\%$ of it at top-3. The information for this task is therefore in the features.

The catalog row is not a ceiling on the task. It bounds what a \emph{single
ranked quantity} can do, and it falls short of $1.0$ because the label is a
threshold test (``$[\mathrm{Fe/H}] > 0.3$'') while a ranking is a distance test:
a star can sit far from the group median without crossing the threshold. A
readout that models the group jointly, the head of
\Cref{tab:headboth}, is not bound by it.

Scoring the narrators by the same rule, crediting any equally-qualifying star,
raises both narrators ($0.332 \to 0.419$ without the head, $0.662 \to 0.756$ with
it) and leaves the head's lead intact: $+0.33 \to +0.34$ ($n{=}1{,}203$ groups,
exact McNemar $p{=}7\times10^{-81}$).

\subsection{The galaxy narrators: token or training?}\label{app:2x2}
The galaxy head narrator differs from the fully fine-tuned narrator in two ways:
it reads the head token, and it is trained with LoRA on a frozen base. To separate
the two, we trained a third narrator on the \emph{same} text with the \emph{same}
LoRA settings and no head token, and we also ran the head narrator with its token
scrambled at test time (each galaxy receives the head token of another test
galaxy). All four narrators describe the same $10{,}677$ test galaxies, decoded
greedily, and are scored as in \Cref{sec:galaxyscore}.

Three things follow. First, the LoRA narrator without the head token is as good
as the one with it: indistinguishable on quiescent galaxies and slightly better
on star-forming ones. Second, the fully fine-tuned narrator is clearly worse on
quiescent galaxies; most of its errors there come from stating that a quiescent
galaxy still forms stars. Third, scrambling the head narrator's token costs it a
little on star-forming galaxies, so the narrator does read the token, but the
information it carries is already available from the data tokens. On galaxies,
the improvement over the fully fine-tuned narrator therefore comes from the
training, not from the head token. The stellar equivalent of this control was not
run (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{The training recipe}\label{app:recipe}
\Cref{tab:recipe} tests the two input changes of \Cref{sec:data} on the galaxy
data with Llama-3.1-8B. \emph{Novel phrasing} is the fraction of generated
descriptions that do not copy a training description (\Cref{app:eval}); low
values mean the narrator recites the templates.

The upper block adds the components one at a time on a $95$k-record subset,
with everything else fixed. Varying the input format raises accuracy on single
objects, but the narrator starts reciting its training text: novel phrasing
falls from $0.40$ to $0.18$ on single objects and from $0.42$ to $0.04$ on pairs,
and pair accuracy drops. Adding the paper sentences reverses this (novel
phrasing $0.39$ and $0.68$) and gives the highest accuracy on every metric.

The lower block, on the full $288$k-record corpus, adds the paper sentences
\emph{without} varying the format. On single objects this does not help: accuracy
is unchanged and copying increases (novel phrasing $0.15 \to 0.06$). Pairs do
improve. This comparison differs from the upper block in corpus size, learning-rate
schedule and the share of intervening text taken from papers, so we read it only
as consistent with the claim that the paper sentences help when the input format
is varied. A one-variable test of the paper sentences without varied formats is
in progress.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{app:eval}
\textbf{Rule-based verifier (single and pair records).} A deterministic program
extracts every numeric and categorical claim from the generated text and checks
it against the object's catalog record. No model is involved.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \emph{Numeric accuracy}: the fraction of stated values whose absolute error
  is within the per-quantity tolerance of \Cref{tab:tol}. A value off by more than
  three times the tolerance counts as a \emph{contradiction}.
\item \emph{Qualitative accuracy} (single): the fraction of class words that
  match the catalog. For stars: evolutionary state (dwarf, subgiant, giant) and
  group-relative classes (metal-poor/rich, fast/slow rotator, young/old,
  luminous/faint, Sun-like). For galaxies: morphology class (early/late disk,
  elliptical, mixed), star-formation state (quiescent, green valley,
  star-forming, starburst) and mass class.
\item \emph{Relational accuracy} (pair): the fraction of comparisons that match the
  catalog. For stars: directional comparatives (hotter, more massive, larger,
  older, higher $\log g$, more luminous, more metal-rich, and their opposites).
  For galaxies: same or different morphology, star-formation-history trend,
  disturbance and density cluster.
\item \emph{Grounded} $=1-$ the fraction of claims not supported by the record.
\item \emph{Novel phrasing} measures whether the narrator writes its own
  sentences or recites training text. For each generated description we find
  the most similar description in the training corpus (MinHash nearest
  neighbour over word $5$-grams) and compute the fraction of the generation's
  $5$-word sequences that appear verbatim in it. A generation \emph{copies} a
  training description if at least half of its $5$-word sequences do. Novel
  phrasing is $1-$ the fraction of generations that copy.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scoring the galaxy task.}\label{sec:galaxyscore} We read three statements
from each galaxy description: the galaxy's shape (disk or spheroid), whether it
still forms stars, and the verdict, i.e., whether the description calls the two in
agreement. Each is read with a short list of words. For the verdict, ``mismatch'',
``disagree'', ``outlier'' or ``unusual'' mark disagreement, and ``concordant'',
``in accord'', ``concur'' or ``agree'' mark agreement. In the training text these
words are exact: every description containing a disagreement word belongs to a
disagreeing galaxy, and every one containing an agreement word to an agreeing
galaxy (\Cref{app:eval}). We report the fraction of galaxies with the correct
verdict, separately for star-forming and quiescent galaxies. The split matters
because $90\%$ of the test galaxies are star-forming, and the errors concentrate in
the quiescent minority, where a narrator must notice that a galaxy has stopped
forming stars. Whenever a narrator's verdict is right, its stated shape and
star-formation state are also right in more than $99.9\%$ of cases.

\textbf{The judge (stellar population records).} The verifier's extractors
expect the template's phrasing, so the population task is also scored by the
judge (Qwen3.5-27B), a text-only model that never sees any token. It reads each group
description against the ground-truth record and is instructed to judge factual
content only, not style. It returns: whether the text names the true odd member
(\emph{named}); whether it attributes it to the correct property
(\emph{property}); whether the population statement describes the typical
members (\emph{covered}); and a list of claims that contradict the record
(\emph{contradictions}). We derive \emph{score} = named \emph{and} property,
and \emph{all correct} = score, covered, and no contradiction. Only groups that
contain an odd member are scored. All narrators are judged on the same groups, so
comparisons are paired (exact McNemar for yes/no outcomes, Wilcoxon for
contradiction counts). The judge is given no numeric tolerance and is stricter
than the verifier: of the numeric contradictions it lists that we could parse, $56$--$60\%$ (depending on the narrator) are within
the verifier's tolerance. Its contradiction counts are therefore an upper bound,
and they inflate more for a narrator that states more numbers.

\textbf{Galaxy agreement task.} Each description is reduced to three statements,
read with fixed word lists taken from the nine single-galaxy templates of the
training text. \emph{Shape}: ``late-type disk'' or ``early-type disk'' (disk),
``elliptical'' or ``lenticular'' (spheroid); general phrases that state the rule,
such as ``spheroidal systems'', are ignored. \emph{Star formation}: the first of
``star-forming'' or ``still forming stars'' (star-forming) and ``quiescent'' or
``has quenched'' (quiescent); the galaxy's own state always precedes the rule
clause, as in ``This disk is quiescent --- \ldots{} disks usually remain
star-forming''. \emph{Verdict}: the words in \Cref{tab:verdictwords}; a
description containing words from both lists, or from neither, has no verdict.
\Cref{tab:verdictwords} checks every word against the ground truth of the
$36{,}654$ single-galaxy training descriptions; the shape and star-formation
rules are likewise correct on $100\%$ of them. A galaxy's verdict is scored
correct if it matches the catalog. No narrator left a galaxy without a verdict.
