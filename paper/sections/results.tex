\textbf{LoRA instead of full fine-tuning.} With the same optimizer schedule, LoRA raises the
score from $0.33$ to $0.51$ on the test groups ($p<10^{-24}$) and from $0.28$ to $0.59$ on
the groups whose odd star was never odd in training ($p<10^{-60}$). This run had not
converged when it was stopped. It finds the odd star more often but makes more contradictions
than the fully fine-tuned narrator ($2.32$ against $1.90$ per group), so what LoRA brings at
this schedule is the identification of the odd star, not a more accurate description overall.

\textbf{The optimizer schedule and training length.} Moving to the schedule of the tool
narrator adds $0.12$ on the test groups ($p<10^{-12}$), only $0.03$ on the never-odd groups
($p=0.14$, not significant), and removes about two thirds of a contradiction per group on
both sets ($p<10^{-37}$). Since the previous run had not converged, we attribute this step to
the schedule and the training length together. Neither recipe was tuned, so a better recipe
for full fine-tuning may exist; LoRA is better without one.

\textbf{The tool.} With the training held fixed, the tool adds $0.03$ on the test groups
($p=0.07$), and $0.13$ on the groups whose odd star was never odd in training ($0.62 \to 0.75$,
$p=5\times10^{-15}$). Part of this gain lies in the groups whose odd star is a binary, $54\%$ of
this set ($0.48 \to 0.64$), and binarity is one of the catalog flags the tool receives. The gain
remains without them ($0.78 \to 0.88$, $n=458$, $p=6\times10^{-6}$). Temperature and surface
gravity are also inputs of the tool, as described above, so we read this result as follows:
when catalog labels are given to the tool as auxiliary data, the narrator uses the returned
token to find outliers for which the training data offers no precedent. We do not separate
the contribution of the learned density from that of the labels, which the tool models jointly.

\subsection{what does the narrator memorize?}\label{sec:memorization}
A narrator trained on templated text, and on the stars it is later tested on
(\Cref{sec:data}), could copy instead of read. Three things could be copied: the
wording of the training descriptions, the values written about a particular
object, and the answer to a reasoning question. We test each where it can be
tested (\Cref{tab:memo}). The tests use different data, so each is read against
its own control, not against the other rows.

\textbf{Wording.} With a varied input format but no paper sentences, the
narrator recites its training descriptions: the fraction of novel descriptions
falls from $0.40$ to $0.18$ on single objects and from $0.42$ to $0.04$ on
pairs. Adding the paper sentences restores it ($0.39$ and $0.68$). Every
narrator in this paper uses both.

\textbf{Values about one object.} On galaxies, where no test galaxy is
described in training, the narrator states about what a non-linear probe on the
same tokens can read. On stars it states more: it is more accurate than the same
probe on most numbers (radius error $0.40$ against $0.82\,R_\odot$), and it
gives the exact temperature (zero Kelvin error) in $35\%$ of its statements, against $0.5\%$
for the probe. Almost every such exact value is written in some training
description of the same star; where none gives it, exact matches fall to the
probe's level. The average error changes little. Part of the stellar
narrator's numeric precision is therefore recall, and its single-star numbers
in \Cref{tab:basemodel} should be read with this in mind.

\subsection{giving the model the numbers as text}\label{sec:text}
If the features carry the signal and the model cannot use it, an obvious
alternative is to skip the features and hand the model the catalog numbers
directly. We test this on the stellar task. Each group is shown as a table with
one row per star, listing its catalog values (temperature, surface gravity,
metallicity, radius, mass, and others) together with the two catalog flags the
tool also receives: whether the star is a binary and whether it belongs to an
open cluster. With these flags the table holds everything the tool is given, so
the comparison is fair for all five kinds of odd star. The model sees two worked
examples and is asked, in words, to name the star that does not belong and the
property it differs in. We test two models: the base model (AstroSage-8B), without our
training, and Qwen3.5-27B, a larger general-purpose model. Each is tested twice:
answering directly, and reasoning step by step before answering.
\Cref{tab:text} compares them with the two narrators on the same $1{,}203$
groups.

\dl{No model that reads the numbers comes close to the narrator with the tool
($0.662$). AstroSage reading the table reaches the second-best score ($0.473$),}\nw{No model that reads the numbers reaches the LoRA narrators ($0.633$ without the tool and
$0.662$ with it; $p<10^{-14}$ for the best text reader against the LoRA narrator without the
tool). AstroSage reading the table reaches the best score among them ($0.473$),}
but by naming most of the group ($82\%$ of the stars): naming the odd star among
them is then no better than chance ($0.92\times$). Asked to reason first, it
\dl{names few stars and scores $0.288$, below the narrator without the tool.}\nw{names few stars and scores $0.288$, below the fully fine-tuned narrator.} The
larger model is more selective: it names the odd star at about the same rate as
the tool relative to the number of stars it names. However, it often gets the
odd star's property wrong, and scores $0.341$ answering directly and $0.324$
\dl{after reasoning, no different from the narrator without the tool ($p{=}0.7$ in
both cases).}\nw{after reasoning, no different from the fully fine-tuned narrator ($p{=}0.7$ in
both cases).} In most groups ($94\%$) the larger model was still reasoning when
it reached the length limit of $4{,}096$ tokens and was made to answer from what
it had, so its reasoning result holds for this budget only. \dl{The gap to the tool
also holds for every group size}\nw{The gap to the LoRA narrators

lets a model describe the stars, but not reliably pick out the one that does
not belong.

\subsection{Templates vs. LLM prose}
Everything above is trained on prose written by the deterministic templater.
The obvious alternative is to let a capable LLM write it, which has the potential for better diversity and fluency. To compare the two, we built an LLM-based corpus: the writer (DeepSeek-V4) was given the same
catalog facts and asked to write the group descriptions, and every claim it made
was checked against the record before the text was accepted. The training text
is, in that sense, true. \Cref{tab:corpus} compares the two corpora with
everything else held fixed: same base model, same prompt, and no tool (both narrators read the data tokens only and are fully fine-tuned). We see that training on the writer's fluent prose makes the narrator invent:
contradictions per group go from $1.9$ to $7.2$, and not one group out of
$1{,}191$ is described fully correctly (odd star and property named, typical members described correctly, and no statement contradicting the record). The training text was verified true; the
model trained on it is not. This is aligned with the finding of \cite{Hegselmann2022TabLLMFC} who showed that template-based serialization outperforms LLM-based serialization for tabular data. The two corpora differ in how much they commit to. Per group description, the
templater writes a median of $21$ words containing $5$ numbers and names $17\%$
of the members; it states the population and the member that does not belong,
and says nothing about the rest. The writer uses $82$ words containing $17$
numbers and names $83\%$ of the members, giving most of them a full quantitative
profile. Training on the second teaches the narrator to do the same. On the test groups,
the narrator trained on writer prose writes a median of $78$ words against $17$, states
$8$ values per group against $1$, and names $72\%$ of the members against
$26\%$. Most of its extra contradictions ($70\%$) are such numeric claims, and
most of those concern members \emph{other} than the outlier, whose values the
template-trained narrator rarely states at all. Per stated value, the two narrators are
contradicted at a similar rate ($0.52$ vs.\ $0.46$): the writer-trained narrator is
wrong more often mainly because it says more. Values for the typical members are hard to read
for both narrators: for either one about $40\%$ of them contradict the record
(verifier). A narrator trained to state them for every member therefore
mostly adds errors. This is not the whole story: on the outlier itself, the writer-trained
narrator's stated values contradict the record far more often ($18\%$ vs.\ $2\%$ of
values, verifier). Verification of the training text does not help in either
case, because the problem is not that the text is false; it is that it asserts
more than the input determines.
