\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{singles and pair questions}
We first test the single and pair questions, comparing two types of Llama-8B models: Llama-3.1 and AstroSage \citep{haan2025_astrosage}, a Llama-3.1 model that was fine-tuned specifically on arXiv astrophysics papers.~\Cref{tab:basemodel} shows the comparison on both datasets. Single and pair descriptions are scored by a rule-based verifier that extracts every numeric and categorical claim from the generated text and checks it against the catalog: \emph{numeric accuracy} is the fraction of stated values within a per-quantity tolerance of the catalog value, and \emph{qualitative} (single) or \emph{relational} (pair) accuracy is the fraction of class words or comparisons that match it (tolerances and definitions in \Cref{app:eval}). While both models show high performance on both tasks, Astrosage retains a small gain over the vanilla model in seven of the eight cells, across both datasets, which implies that textual domain knowledge is helpful for those tasks. Therefore, the Astrosage model was chosen for further analysis. 
\subsection{scientific reasoning tasks} Single and pair questions consists of description and simple comparisons and requires almost no reasoning beyond the basic properties. It is also similar to the protein description tasks as in  \cite{Dotan2026_betadescribe}, and therefore it is not surprising that an LLM performs well on such questions. Next, we test the performance on questions that requires some form of scientific reasoning. As mentioned in~\Cref{sec:data}, those are anomaly detection for the stellar dataset and irregular morphology-SFH correlation in the galaxy dataset.~\Cref{tab:headboth} shows the results of those two tasks using both a template-based verifier and an LLM judge (Qwen3.5-27B). The \emph{no head} column presents the basic model, with no extra information, and the performance is poor. Before adding anything to the model, we check whether this is a failure of information. A cross-fit linear probe on the same aligned features, asked to rank the members of a group, matches what the true catalog values achieve (rank-1 $0.660$ from the shared subspace and $0.686$ from the full feature, against $0.660$ for the catalog itself; \Cref{sec:appendix}). The signal the task needs is present in the features the model reads, so the failure is in the readout and not in the representation. The \emph{+ head} column shows the performance with the addition of an extra single token that comes from a dedicated non-LLM model that is meant to highlight the relevant information. In the stellar case this is a flow matching model that was trained to learn the joint distribution of the aligned features and catalog labels, conditioned on the rest of the group. In the galaxy case, this is a cross-modal predictor that is trained to predict one modality from the other. We note that adding this single token significantly improves the performance on both tasks.

$^{\ast}$~The galaxy task is imbalanced, and the imbalance decides how it must be
scored. A morphology--SFH mismatch means a \emph{spheroid} among star-forming
galaxies but a \emph{disk} among quiescent ones, and $90\%$ of the test galaxies
are star-forming. A narrator that reads morphology alone and learns ``spheroid
$\Rightarrow$ mismatch'' is therefore right on the majority, backwards on the
minority, and still looks excellent on the pooled sample. Whether the text states
the right verdict does not catch this. Trained on the same prose, the model
without the token calls $0.923$ of the mismatching galaxies correctly and the
model with it $0.973$, so both use the right words. Instead, we score each galaxy by the model's own
graded confidence, the log-probability of opening its description with a mismatch
framing rather than a neutral one (no fitted weights). We compute the AUC separately within each
star-formation stratum and report the minimum ($0.50$ = chance). \Cref{fig:concordance}
shows why. On the star-forming majority every arm is high. On the quiescent
minority, the two arms without the token are at or below chance and only the arm
with the token is above it. Pooled, the shortcut learner posts the best score of
all ($0.885$), which is why we never report pooled AUC for this task.

\subsection{is it the token, or the retraining that came with it?}
The head arms differ from the no-head arms in more than the token: they are
trained with LoRA on a frozen base, while the no-head arms are full fine-tunes.
A reader is entitled to ask whether the gain is the token at all. The test that
does not depend on the training regime is to take the \emph{same trained model}
and destroy the token at generation time, leaving everything else untouched.
\Cref{tab:ablate} does this two ways: replacing the token with zeros, and
scrambling its contents across the group so that it carries the same statistics
but no longer describes the star it is attached to.

Scrambling the token costs $0.056$ and zeroing it costs
$0.101$, both significant. The token is not a flag whose mere presence helps:
what it \emph{contains} is read and used. The galaxy track shows the same thing:
removing the cross-modal token at test time drops the trained narrator from
$0.628$ to $0.461$ (\Cref{tab:headboth}).
The gap between no
token at all ($0.332$) and a zeroed token ($0.561$) still mixes the token being
there with the LoRA regime that came with it. To separate them, we trained a model with LoRA and no token on the galaxy dataset. 
It reaches a min-stratum AUC of $0.498$
($[0.462, 0.535]$, indistinguishable from chance), against $0.442$ for the
full fine-tuned no-token arm and $0.628$ with the token. This implies that the token,
not the fine-tuning pipeline, is what carries the result. \Cref{sec:appendix} gives the
complete $2\times2$. To save compute, we conducted this experiment only on the galaxy dataset.

\subsection{is this reasoning, or memorization?}
Since each star creates multiple training prose, each time with different labels and framing, the datasets are not split-aware, sample-wise: $99.2\%$ of the stars in the
stellar test fold appear in \emph{some} training description. That is symmetric across
experiments, so the paired contrasts above are fair, but it leaves an obvious
objection: the head arm might be recalling which star was called unusual
during training rather than working it out.

We test this directly, on the stellar track. From the test fold we keep only
groups whose odd member was \emph{never} an odd member anywhere in training:
the star may have been described many times, but never as the one that stands
out. Only $2.3\%$ of candidate groups survive that filter, leaving $1{,}000$.
If the head is recalling, it should lose most of its advantage here. Table~\ref{tab:adversarial} shows this experiment. The advantage does not shrink where memorisation is
impossible; it grows. The head is worth $+0.469$ on this set against
$+0.330$ on the full fold, and the two arms move in opposite directions when
they are moved onto it: the arm without the head falls ($0.332 \to 0.279$)
while the arm with it does not. That is the opposite of what a memorization
explanation predicts.

\subsection{giving the model the numbers as text does not help}
If the features carry the signal and the model cannot use it, an obvious
alternative is to skip the features and hand the model the catalog numbers
directly. We test this on the stellar dataset only, where the group task
has a written form: the same base model is shown each group as an indexed table
of numeric fields (more fields than the flow matching head ever sees, as each prose uses a subset of the fields) and asked, in
words, to name the member that stands out and the property it differs on. We
also test a version that is told to reason through the stars one at a time
before committing to an answer. \Cref{tab:text} shows both against the two
latent arms on the same groups.
At first sight, the text arm looks competitive on naming the odd member alone ($0.592$)
but it names $68\%$ of the group and never stops generating, so the rate is
mostly hedging: corrected for how much it names, it is below chance
($0.87\times$). On \textbf{score}, where it must also name the right property,
it scores $0.331$ against $0.320$ for the latent tokens, and both are less than half the head's
$0.652$. Reasoning first makes the arm commit ($18\%$ of the group named
instead of $68\%$, and it terminates) but does not rescue it: $0.224$, the
lowest of any arm that finishes. Reading the same numbers as text is therefore
not successful, with or without explicit reasoning.

\subsection{Templates vs. LLM prose}
Everything above is trained on prose written by the deterministic templater.
The obvious alternative is to let a capable LLM write it, which has the potential for better diversity and fluency. To compare the two, we built an LLM-based corpus: DeepSeek-V4 was given the same
catalog facts and asked to write the group descriptions, and every claim it made
was checked against the record before the text was accepted. The training text
is, in that sense, true. \Cref{tab:corpus} compares the two corpora with
everything else held fixed: same base model, same prompt, and no head (both narrators read the latent tokens only and are fully fine-tuned). We see that training on fluent LLM prose makes the model invent:
contradictions per group go from $1.9$ to $7.2$, and not one group out of
$1{,}191$ is described fully correctly. The training text was verified true; the
model trained on it is not. This is aligned with the finding of \cite{Hegselmann2022TabLLMFC} who showed that template-based serialization outperforms LLM-based serialization for tabular data. The two corpora differ in how much they commit to. Per group description, the
templater writes a median of $21$ words containing $5$ numbers and names $17\%$
of the members; it states the population and the member that does not belong,
and says nothing about the rest. The teacher-LLM writes $82$ words containing $17$
numbers and names $83\%$ of the members, giving most of them a full quantitative
profile. Training on the second teaches the model to do the same. On the test groups,
the model trained on LLM prose writes a median of $78$ words against $17$, states
$8$ values per group against $1$, and names $72\%$ of the members against
$26\%$. Most of its extra contradictions ($70\%$) are such numeric claims, and
most of those concern members \emph{other} than the outlier, whose values the
template-trained model rarely states at all. Per stated value, the two models are
contradicted at a similar rate ($0.52$ vs.\ $0.46$): the LLM-prose model is
wrong more often mainly because it says more. Values for the typical members are hard to read
for both models: in either arm about $40\%$ of them contradict the record
(rule verifier). A model trained to state them for every member therefore
mostly adds errors. This is not the whole story: on the outlier itself, the LLM-prose
model's stated values contradict the record far more often ($18\%$ vs.\ $2\%$ of
values, rule verifier). Verification of the training text does not help in either
case, because the problem is not that the text is false; it is that it asserts
more than the input determines.
