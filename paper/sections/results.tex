\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{singles and pair questions}
We first test the single and pair questions, comparing two types of Llama-8B models: Llama-3.1 and AstroSage \citep{haan2025_astrosage}, a Llama-3.1 model that was fine-tuned specifically on arXiv astrophysics papers.~\Cref{tab:basemodel} shows the comparison on both datasets. While both models show high performance on both tasks, Astrosage retains a small gain over the vanilla model in seven of the eight cells, across both datasets, which implies that textual domain knowledge is helpful for those tasks. Therefore, the Astrosage model was chosen for further analysis. 
\subsection{scientific reasoning tasks} Single and pair questions consists of description and simple comparisons and requires almost no reasoning beyond the basic properties. It is also similar to the protein description tasks as in  \cite{Dotan2026_betadescribe}, and therefore it is not surprising that an LLM performs well on such questions. Next, we test the performance on questions that requires some form of scientific reasoning. As mentioned in~\Cref{sec:data}, those are anomaly detection for the stellar dataset and irregular morphology-SFH correlation in the galaxy dataset.~\Cref{tab:headboth} shows the results of those two tasks using both a template-based verifier and an LLM judge (Qwen3.5-27B). The \emph{no head} column presents the basic model, with no extra information, and the performance is poor. Before adding anything to the model, we check whether this is a failure of information. A cross-fit linear probe on the same aligned features, asked to rank the members of a group, matches what the true catalog values achieve (rank-1 $0.660$ from the shared subspace and $0.686$ from the full feature, against $0.660$ for the catalog itself; \Cref{sec:appendix}). The signal the task needs is present in the features the model reads, so the failure is in the readout and not in the representation. The \emph{+ head} column shows the performance with the addition of an extra single token that comes from a dedicated non-LLM model that is meant to highlight the relevant information. In the stellar case this is a flow matching model that was trained to learn the joint distribution of the aligned features and catalog labels, conditioned on the rest of the group. In the galaxy case, this is a cross-modal predictor that is trained to predict one modality from the other. We note that adding this single token significantly improves the performance on both tasks.

``spheroid $\Rightarrow$ mismatch'', which is right for the $90\%$ star-forming
majority and backwards for the quiescent minority.


\subsection{is it the token, or the retraining that came with it?}
The head arms differ from the no-head arms in more than the token: they are
trained with LoRA on a frozen base, while the no-head arms are full fine-tunes.
A reader is entitled to ask whether the gain is the token at all. The test that
does not depend on the training regime is to take the \emph{same trained model}
and destroy the token at generation time, leaving everything else untouched.
\Cref{tab:ablate} does this two ways: replacing the token with zeros, and
scrambling its contents across the group so that it carries the same statistics
but no longer describes the star it is attached to.

damaged at generation time --- same weights, same training regime, only the
token changes. $p$ is exact McNemar against the intact arm.}
\label{tab:ablate}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule

\midrule
no head token at all        & 0.332 & $2{\times}10^{-70}$ \\
\;+ head token, zeroed      & 0.561 & $2{\times}10^{-11}$ \\
\;+ head token, scrambled   & 0.606 & $2{\times}10^{-5}$ \\
\;+ head token, intact      & \textbf{0.662} & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{What it says.} Scrambling the token costs $0.056$ and zeroing it costs
$0.101$, both significant. The token is not a flag whose mere presence helps:
what it \emph{contains} is read and used. We quote the scrambled row as the
headline, because zeroing pushes the input off the distribution the model was
trained on while scrambling does not. The galaxy track shows the same thing ---
removing the cross-modal token at test time drops the trained narrator from
$0.628$ to $0.461$ (\Cref{tab:headboth}).

This settles the content of the token but not its presence: the gap between no
token at all ($0.332$) and a zeroed token ($0.561$) still mixes the token being
there with the LoRA regime that came with it. To separate them we trained the
missing cell on the galaxy track --- same prose, same LoRA regime, no token ---
where the cost is affordable. It reaches a min-stratum AUC of $0.498$
($[0.462, 0.535]$, indistinguishable from chance), against $0.442$ for the
full fine-tuned no-token arm and $0.628$ with the token. The training regime on
its own buys nothing on the interaction task; both no-token arms sit at chance
on the minority stratum and learn the morphology shortcut instead. The token,
not the regime, is what carries the result. \Cref{sec:appendix} gives the
complete $2\times2$.

\subsection{is it reasoning, or is it recall?}
The stellar prose corpus was not built split-aware: $99.2\%$ of the stars in the
test fold appear in \emph{some} training description. That is symmetric across
arms, so the paired contrasts above are fair, but it leaves an obvious
objection --- the head arm might be recalling which star was called unusual
during training rather than working it out.

We test this directly, on the stellar track. From the test fold we keep only
groups whose odd member was \emph{never} an odd member anywhere in training:
the star may have been described many times, but never as the one that stands
out. Only $2.3\%$ of candidate groups survive that filter, leaving $1{,}000$.
If the head is recalling, it should lose most of its advantage here.

\bottomrule

\textbf{What it says.} The advantage does not shrink where memorisation is
impossible --- it grows. The head is worth $+0.469$ on this set against
$+0.330$ on the full fold, and the two arms move in opposite directions when
they are moved onto it: the arm without the head falls ($0.332 \to 0.279$)
while the arm with it does not. That is the opposite of what a recall
explanation predicts. We wrote the prediction down before running it.

One caveat on reading the table. This set is deliberately harder than the full
fold and its item mix is different, so only the within-set gap is quotable:
compare $0.748$ to $0.279$, not to the $0.662$ of \Cref{tab:headboth}. Groups
with no odd member are excluded throughout, here and everywhere else in the
paper.

\subsection{giving the model the numbers as text does not help}
If the features carry the signal and the model cannot use it, an obvious
alternative is to skip the features and hand the model the catalog numbers
directly. We test this on the \emph{stellar} dataset only, where the group task
has a written form: the same base model is shown each group as an indexed table
of numeric fields --- more fields than the head ever sees --- and asked, in
words, to name the member that stands out and the property it differs on. We
also test a version that is told to reason through the stars one at a time
before committing to an answer. \Cref{tab:text} shows both against the two
latent arms on the same groups.

\textbf{What it says.} The text arm looks competitive on the raw rate ($0.592$)
but it names $68\%$ of the group and never stops generating, so the rate is
mostly hedging: corrected for how much it names, it is below chance
($0.87\times$). On \textbf{found}, where it must also name the right property,
it scores $0.331$ against $0.320$ for the latent tokens --- the two are
statistically indistinguishable --- and both are less than half the head's
$0.652$. Reasoning first makes the arm commit ($18\%$ of the group named
instead of $68\%$, and it terminates) but does not rescue it: $0.224$, the
lowest of any arm that finishes. Reading the same numbers as text is therefore
not a route to the group task, with or without explicit reasoning.

\subsection{the head has to match the question}
The two heads are not interchangeable, and we know because we tried. Our first
attempt on galaxies was to port the stellar head unchanged: a density model over
the aligned features, scoring each object by how unlikely it is. On stars that
is the right question --- the odd member of a group really is a rare object. On
galaxies it found nothing.

\Cref{tab:wronghead} shows why we abandoned it. For each axis we compare the
density head reading the full galaxy features against the \emph{same} head given
only the catalog labels. If the features carried usable signal for this head,
the first column should beat the second. On eight of the nine axes it is worse,
including the cross-modal axis this paper cares about ($0.589$ against
$0.662$). The features are not adding anything the head can use.

\textbf{What it says.} This is not a broken model or a useless representation:
the \emph{same} galaxy features, read by a cross-modal predictor instead of a
density model, support the task at $0.628$ (\Cref{tab:headboth}). What changed
is the question the head asks. A density model scores \emph{rarity} --- how
unlikely is this object. The galaxy question is \emph{disagreement} --- does the
image agree with the star-formation history. A star-forming spheroid is a
perfectly ordinary galaxy that happens to sit on the wrong side of a boundary,
so it is not rare, and a likelihood cannot see it however well it is trained.
The stellar anomalies, by contrast, genuinely are rare.

So the recipe is not ``attach a head''. It is ``attach a head that computes the
quantity the question turns on''. Deciding which one that is --- rarity, or
agreement between two views --- is a modelling choice made before any training,
and getting it wrong costs everything: the same pipeline, the same data and the
same narrator produce a result at chance.

\subsection{verified templates beat fluent LLM prose}
Everything above is trained on prose written by the deterministic templater.
The obvious alternative is to let a capable LLM write it, which reads far better
and is much less work. We built that corpus too: DeepSeek-V4 was given the same
catalog facts and asked to write the group descriptions, and every claim it made
was checked against the record before the text was accepted. The training text
is, in that sense, true. \Cref{tab:corpus} compares the two corpora with
everything else held fixed --- same base model, same prompt, no head.

\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule

\midrule
template (ours)       & \textbf{0.333} & \textbf{0.573} & \textbf{1.88} & \textbf{0.128} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{What it says.} Training on fluent LLM prose makes the model invent:
contradictions per group go from $1.9$ to $7.2$, and not one group out of
$1{,}191$ is described fully correctly. The training text was verified true; the
model trained on it is not.

The two corpora differ in how much they commit to. Per group description, the
templater writes a median of $21$ words containing $5$ numbers and names $17\%$
of the members --- it states the population and the member that does not belong,
and says nothing about the rest. The teacher writes $82$ words containing $17$
numbers and names $83\%$ of the members, giving most of them a full quantitative
profile. Training on the second teaches the model to produce that profile for
every object in every group. At generation time on a group it has not seen, the
tokens do not support reading off five exact values per object, so it supplies
them anyway. Verification of the training text does not help here, because the
problem is not that the text is false --- it is that it asserts more than the
input determines.
