\section{Data and Model}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Terminology.} We use six names throughout. The \emph{narrator} is
the 8B language model we train: it reads an object's data tokens and answers questions
about the object. The \emph{head} is a small model, not a language model,
trained separately to compute one quantity that a question depends on
(\Cref{sec:results}); its output enters the narrator as one extra token, the
\emph{head token}. The \emph{templater} is the deterministic program that writes
our training text from catalog facts, and the \emph{writer} (DeepSeek-V4) is the
LLM we use in its place in one comparison. Generated text is scored by the
\emph{verifier}, a program that checks every stated fact against the catalog, and
by the \emph{judge} (Qwen3.5-27B), an LLM that reads each description against
the ground truth.

We test two different astrophysical datasets, representing different domains and physical properties. The first dataset is a \emph{stellar} multimodal dataset comprising 190,091 stars, observed by $5$ different modalities coming from different telescopes - \emph{Kepler}\citep{kepler:mission}, TESS\citep{tess:mission}, LAMOST\citep{lamost:survey}, APOGEE\citep{apogee:mission}, and Gaia\citep{gaia:mission}. Each modality is processed through a specific encoder, and the modalities are aligned with a model that factorizes the latent space into shared and specific subspaces. The result is one shared feature and $5$ per-modality features, each projected to the token embedding space of the LLM as its own small group of tokens. The second dataset is galaxies from the COSMOS-Web survey \citep{Casey2023_cosmos-web}, including two modalities: images and star-formation history (SFH). Similar to the stellar dataset, we use a pretrained model that aligns the two modalities (here using CLIP \cite{Radford2021_CLIP}) and project the frozen features into the LLM's token embedding. Notably, the two datasets are different in many aspects: number and type of modalities, pretrained models, and scientific questions. Using the two datasets in the same pipeline highlights general vs data-specific conclusions.\\  
\textbf{training prose}. One of the challenges in this pipeline is creating the training dataset. This should include ground-truth prose per object. Contrary to other fields, in astrophysics data is usually stored only in numerical catalogs, so one needs to create the prose from scratch. Our main method is a \emph{template-based} method: a deterministic program that picks a subset of facts from the catalog, chooses a phrasing from a bank of templates, and
writes prose. Every sentence is checked against the record by the verifier before it enters the corpus. The
check is per-fact: it guarantees that the facts a description states are true of the object, not
that the description mentions every unusual object in a group. Both datasets share two types of prose: description of a single object (single), and comparison between pairs (pair). We also test deeper reasoning capabilities with two additional tasks. In the stellar dataset we use population prose: a group of $N$ objects with description of the typical members, and a callout
of the one that does not belong and on which property. In the galaxy dataset, the task is to judge whether a galaxy's shape agrees with its star-formation history, a relation between its two modalities. In~\Cref{sec:results} we compare the template-based method with a second method: an \emph{LLM-based} generation, where the writer produces the population prose from the same catalog facts, and every claim is verified.

\textbf{Objects, facts and descriptions.} The stellar set has $190{,}091$
stars, of which we evaluate on a held-out set of $19{,}010$. Because every star is
described in many training records and the stellar training text was not built
split by split, $99.2\%$ of these evaluation stars appear in \emph{some} training
description; \Cref{sec:memorization} tests whether this matters. The galaxy set
has $137{,}696$ galaxies, split $80/10/10$ into training, validation and test
galaxies, and all galaxy training text is written about training galaxies only,
so the test galaxies are never seen in training.

Each star's catalog record holds $13$ numbers ($T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$,
$[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$, mass, radius, age, luminosity, distance, parallax, Gaia's
astrometric quality index RUWE, apparent and absolute brightness, and rotation
period) and categorical facts: evolutionary state (dwarf, subgiant, giant); how
the star compares with the rest of the sample (e.g., among the most metal-rich
quarter, or hotter, older or faster-rotating than most); its Galactic disk
population and $\alpha$-element abundance; its activity level; and flags for
binarity, planet hosts, flares and open-cluster membership. Each galaxy's record
holds stellar mass, star-formation rate and state (quiescent to starburst),
redshift, S\'ersic index (how centrally concentrated its light is), visual
morphology and disturbance, formation age, and the shape of its star-formation
history (rising or falling, recent activity, quenching, formation time).

The templater writes each description by sampling, independently, which facts
to state (a random three to six), a style, and a framing. For stars it also adds,
each with probability one half, a chemistry block (disk population,
$\alpha$-abundance) and an activity block (chromospheric activity, variability
amplitude). Stars have three styles (natural prose, observation log, bullet
list) and four framings (plain description, outlier flag, comparison with other
stars, comparison with the Sun). Galaxies have four styles (technical paper,
observation log, teaching text, concise summary), four framings (plain
description, comparison with the galaxy population, comparison with the
star-forming main sequence, outlier flag) and three lengths. On stars, each fact
is then worded by drawing from a bank of alternative phrasings ($917$
alternatives at $141$ points in the text); the galaxy templater has one wording
per fact and style, so its variety comes from the facts, style, framing and
length. A star is described about five times, each time with different facts
and words (\Cref{fig:example}). The result is highly diverse: every stellar
single and pair description is unique, and $99.9\%$ remain unique with all
numbers masked; for galaxies, $99.9\%$ of single descriptions are unique and
$70\%$ remain so with numbers masked. In total, the stellar corpus has $1.93$M
records ($1.01$M single, $0.69$M pair, $0.23$M population); each population
record is a group of $4$ to $32$ stars with an odd member on one of six
properties. The galaxy corpus has $288$k records ($90$k single, $198$k pair).
The galaxy agreement task uses a separate, deliberately narrow corpus of
$45{,}817$ records (three phrasings per case, also from training galaxies only)
in which galaxies whose morphology and star-formation history disagree are
oversampled to $33\%$.

\textbf{Training recipe.} A narrator trained on templated text can learn to
reproduce the templates instead of reading the data. We counter this with two
changes to the input. First, we vary its format: an object's data tokens appear
alone, after a question or a short passage, separated from the description by
intervening text, or among the tokens of several other objects together with a
request to describe one of them. Second, part of the intervening text consists of
real sentences from astrophysics papers, filtered so that none states a relation
between properties we later test. The two are needed together
(\Cref{app:recipe}). Varying the format alone improves accuracy but makes the
narrator copy training descriptions almost verbatim; adding the paper sentences
removes that copying, and accuracy improves further. Population records are always
presented as the data tokens alone. The recipe was chosen on the galaxy data and
reused unchanged for stars.
