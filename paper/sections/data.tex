\section{Data and Model}
\label{sec:data}
\textbf{Terminology.} We use six names throughout. The \emph{narrator} is
the 8B language model (AstroSage-8B; \cite{haan2025_astrosage}) we train: it reads an object's data tokens and answers questions
about the object. The \emph{tool} is a small model, not a language model,
trained separately to compute, for every object in a question, a quantity that
the question depends on, using auxiliary information (\Cref{sec:ltu}). It is run on every input, and its
output enters the narrator as one extra token per object, the \emph{tool token},
which the narrator is trained to read; a narrator that reads it is a \emph{tool
narrator}. The stellar tool also receives catalog labels as inputs, and narrators without a tool do not. The \emph{templater} is the deterministic program that writes
our training text from catalog facts, and the \emph{writer} (DeepSeek-V4) is the
LLM we use in its place in one comparison. Generated text is scored by the
\emph{verifier}, a program that checks every stated fact against the catalog, and
by the \emph{judge} (Qwen3.5-27B), an LLM that reads each description against
the ground truth.

We test two different astrophysical datasets, representing different domains and physical properties. The first dataset is a \emph{stellar} multimodal dataset comprising 190,091 stars, observed by $5$ different modalities coming from different telescopes - \emph{Kepler}\citep{kepler:mission}, TESS\citep{tess:mission}, LAMOST\citep{lamost:survey}, APOGEE\citep{apogee:mission}, and Gaia\citep{gaia:mission}. Each modality is processed through a specific encoder, and the modalities are aligned with a model that factorizes the latent space into shared and specific subspaces. The result is one shared feature and $5$ per-modality features, each projected to the token embedding space of the LLM as its own small group of tokens. The second dataset is galaxies from the COSMOS-Web survey \citep{Casey2023_cosmos-web}, including two modalities: images and star-formation history (SFH). Similar to the stellar dataset, we use a pretrained model that aligns the two modalities (here using CLIP \cite{Radford2021_CLIP}) and project the frozen features into the LLM's token embedding. Notably, the two datasets are different in many aspects: number and type of modalities, pretrained models, scientific questions, and what their test sets hold out (below). Using the two datasets in the same pipeline highlights general vs data-specific conclusions.\\  
\textbf{training prose}. One of the challenges in this pipeline is creating the training dataset. This should include ground-truth prose per object. Contrary to other fields, in astrophysics data is usually stored only in numerical catalogs, so one needs to create the prose from scratch. Our main method is a \emph{template-based} method: a deterministic program that picks a subset of facts from the catalog, chooses a phrasing from a bank of templates, and
writes prose. Every sentence is checked against the record by the verifier before it enters the corpus. The
check is per-fact: it guarantees that the facts a description states are true of the object.
Both datasets share two types of prose: description of a single object (single), and comparison between pairs (pair).
We also test deeper reasoning capabilities with two additional tasks, and this is where the datasets diverge.
In the stellar dataset, we use population prose: a group of $N$ objects with a description of the typical members, and a callout
of the ones that do not belong and on which property. In the galaxy dataset, the task is to judge whether a galaxy's shape agrees with
its star-formation history, a relation between its two modalities. Those additional tasks represents real scientific tasks:
anomaly detection and unusual cross modal correlation.

\textbf{Objects, facts and descriptions.} The two datasets are split in
different ways. The galaxy
set has $137{,}696$ galaxies, split $80/10/10$ into training, validation and
test galaxies, so the galaxy test set holds out the objects themselves. The stellar set has $190{,}091$ stars and is designed differently:
for each sample we create many different descriptions, with different physical labels,
which results in a dataset of $1.93$M unique
proses. We then split the dataset per-description.
This matches a common scientific practice of looking for 
\emph{new information about known samples}. For example, the stars of the \emph{Kepler} field are
fixed and already catalogued, and the
questions of interest are new classifications or new comparisons among them, not descriptions of stars
never observed. However, from a machine learning prespective, this kind of split raises a concern
that a model would memorize
the samples themselves. We address those concerns in \Cref{sec:memorization}.

The stellar catalog record holds $13$ physical labels ($T_\mathrm{eff}$, $\log g$,
$[\mathrm{Fe/H}]$, mass, radius, age, luminosity, distance, parallax, Gaia's
astrometric quality index RUWE, apparent and absolute brightness, and rotation
period) and categorical facts: evolutionary state (dwarf, subgiant, giant); how
the star compares with the rest of the sample (e.g., among the most metal-rich
quarter, or hotter, older or faster-rotating than most); its Galactic disk
population and $\alpha$-element abundance; its activity level; and flags for
binarity, planet hosts, flares and open-cluster membership. Each galaxy's record
holds stellar mass, star-formation rate and state (quiescent to starburst),
redshift, S\'ersic index (how centrally concentrated its light is), visual
morphology and disturbance, formation age, and the shape of its star-formation
history (rising or falling, recent activity, quenching, formation time).

Stars and galaxies are described by the same kind of templater. For each
description it independently samples which facts to state (three to six), a
writing style and a framing (a plain description, an outlier flag, or a
comparison with a reference population), so every object is described several
times, each time differently (\Cref{fig:example}). The two domains differ in
where the remaining variety comes from: stellar descriptions draw each fact's
wording from a bank of $917$ alternative phrasings and may add chemistry and
activity blocks, while galaxy descriptions use one wording per fact and style
and vary in length instead. The resulting text is diverse: with all numbers
masked, $99.9\%$ of stellar descriptions and $70\%$ of single-galaxy
descriptions are still unique. The stellar corpus has $1.93$M records ($1.01$M
single, $0.69$M pair, $0.23$M population, where a population is a group of $4$
to $32$ stars with one odd member); the galaxy corpus has $288$k ($90$k single,
$198$k pair), plus a separate $46$k-record corpus for the cross modal correlation task.
\Cref{app:templater} gives the full details.

\textbf{Training.} The physics encoders are frozen, and we compute their embeddings once.
An adapter maps each modality's embedding to $4$ tokens in the language model's
input space; it is a two-layer MLP per modality (\Cref{fig:arch}). The adapter is
always trained. The language model is trained in one of two ways. Full
fine-tuning updates all its weights. LoRA freezes them and trains low-rank
matrices (rank $16$) on the attention query and value projections. The tool is
trained beforehand and stays frozen. The loss is next-token cross-entropy on the
final description only.
\Cref{app:recipes} gives the learning rates and schedules.

\textbf{Data augmentation.} A model trained on templated text can memorize the
templates rather than learn to read the data. To prevent this, we randomize the
input format of each training example. The data tokens are placed (i) alone,
(ii) after a question or a short passage, (iii) separated from the description by
intervening text, or (iv) among the tokens of other objects, with an instruction
to describe one of them. Part of the intervening text is drawn from astrophysics
papers, excluding sentences that state any relation evaluated in this work.
Population records always use format (i). The augmentation was tuned on the
galaxy data and applied unchanged to the stellar data (\Cref{app:recipe}).
